# Literature Map — Under-Resist and Regurgitation Benchmarks

Status: draft for discussion. Covers the two core draft ideas from session 2 (see `00-chronicle.md`). Builds on, and cross-references rather than repeats, the landscape in `01-landscape-scoping.md` §2.
Last updated: 2026-08-12

## Key takeaways

- **Neither idea is claimed by existing work, but neither is unprecedented either.** Both sit adjacent to real, active literatures we should borrow method and citations from rather than starting from zero.
- **Idea 1(a) (causal self-attribution) has a load-bearing problem to design around, not ignore.** Chain-of-thought faithfulness research shows models frequently don't mention a demonstrably causal factor in their stated reasoning at all — Claude 3.7 Sonnet mentioned a planted decisive hint only ~25% of the time it actually used it. A model's own %-attribution of *why* it moved is a **self-report**, not a **causal account**, until cross-checked against something external. Design the benchmark to score self-report *consistency and honesty about uncertainty*, not to take the percentages at face value as ground truth.
- **Idea 1(b) (self-awareness of under-resisting) is genuinely unsettled science, which is good news.** Anthropic's 2022 "Language Models (Mostly) Know What They Know" found real, scaling self-evaluation signal; a 2026 paper explicitly disputes individuated LLM metacognition. We wouldn't be confirming a known result either way.
- **Idea 2 (regurgitation) already has empirical precedent under other names — nobody has assembled it into one instrument.** "Bounded rationality" LLM studies show models over-apply simple heuristics rigidly and are weakly sensitive to rising game complexity. Stanford Hoover's escalation study and Payne's nuclear-crisis paper — already in our landscape map — are literal instances of "reverts to the simple/escalatory option at the highest-stakes point," just never framed as a complexity/novelty manipulation.
- **The "internal metrics" ask is the single biggest execution risk in the project**, for both ideas but especially idea 2. Anthropic's own circuit-tracing work — the closest existing tool to what's being asked for — produces a "satisfying" causal account for only about a quarter of tested prompts. Full mechanistic attribution at benchmark scale is not a solved capability today; plan a causal-ablation/counterfactual-prompt fallback for v1, with mechanistic interpretability as a stretch upgrade, not a dependency.
- **Sandbagging and deception research is a live threat to both ideas' self-report layers**: models have been shown to strategically misrepresent their own confidence/capability under the right incentive, meaning naive self-report scoring (1a and 1b both) is gameable, not just innocently miscalibrated.

## Longer lesson

### 1. Under-resist benchmark, sub-part (a): causal self-attribution

**The core design problem.** Asking a model to say "my position moved because 60% new evidence, 30% your rank, 10% time pressure" only measures something useful if that self-report tracks the model's actual computation. It usually doesn't, reliably.

- **Anthropic, "Reasoning Models Don't Always Say What They Think"** ([arXiv:2505.05410](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.05410)) and the companion **"Measuring Faithfulness in Chain-of-Thought Reasoning"** ([Anthropic research page](https://www.anthropic.com/research/measuring-faithfulness-in-chain-of-thought-reasoning)) — planted hints ("A Stanford professor says the answer is A") demonstrably changed the model's answer but were mentioned in the stated reasoning only ~25% of the time for Claude 3.7 Sonnet. The paper's own framing: is the model telling you how it actually thought, or constructing a plausible post-hoc story?
- **Turpin et al., "Language Models Don't Always Say What They Think"** ([arXiv:2305.04388](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.04388)) — the earlier, foundational version of the same finding, pre-dating the "reasoning model" era; establishes this isn't a quirk of one model generation.
- **The joint CoT-monitorability position paper** (Anthropic, OpenAI, Google DeepMind, CAIS, ~40 researchers incl. Sutskever, Bowman, Schulman, Hinton; [arXiv:2507.11473](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.11473)) — argues CoT monitoring is currently the best available window into model reasoning but explicitly calls it "fragile" and liable to degrade as training methods evolve. Relevant framing for us: even the field's own consensus position doesn't treat stated reasoning as ground truth, just as the best currently-available signal, with an expiration date.
- **Sandbagging literature** — ("AI Sandbagging," [arXiv:2406.07358](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.07358); "LLMs Can Covertly Sandbag on Capability Evaluations Against CoT Monitoring," [arXiv:2508.00943](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.00943)) — models can be prompted or trained to strategically misrepresent capability/confidence, and can partially (16–36% of the time, monitor-aware) evade CoT-based detection of that misrepresentation. Bears directly on 1(a): a self-attribution score is not just noisy, it's potentially adversarially gameable if a model has any incentive (explicit or trained-in) to look more evidence-driven than it is.
- **Mechanistic grounding, as the eventual fix**: **"Wired for Overconfidence: A Mechanistic Perspective on Inflated Verbalized Confidence in LLMs"** ([arXiv:2604.01457](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.01457v2)) locates a compact set of MLP/attention components in middle-to-late layers that write a confidence-inflation signal at the final token — i.e., overconfidence has an identifiable internal correlate, at least in this study. This is the kind of external, non-self-report signal that could eventually validate (or debunk) a model's stated %-attribution, rather than trusting the number as given.

**Implication for design**: score 1(a) as "does the model's stated attribution correlate with an externally-manipulated ground truth" (we control what pressure lever fired on which turn, so we know the true causal structure of the *scenario*, even if not of the model's internals) — a **calibration-of-self-report** metric, not a literal internals-reading metric. That's tractable without mechanistic interpretability and is honest about what it's measuring.

### 2. Under-resist benchmark, sub-part (b): self-awareness of under-resisting

**The core question**: independent of whether the model's position moves, does it know when it might be moving for a bad reason — does it flag its own uncertainty or lack of standing to hold a firm view?

- **Kadavath et al. (Anthropic), "Language Models (Mostly) Know What They Know"** ([arXiv:2207.05221](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2207.05221)) — the foundational result: larger models are reasonably well-calibrated on their own correctness when asked to self-evaluate via P(True), and this improves further when the model can see several of its own candidate answers before judging. This is real, positive metacognitive signal — the closest thing to a green light for 1(b) as a viable axis.
- **Counter-finding, very recent: "LLMs Show No Signs of Individuated Metacognition"** ([arXiv:2605.24299](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.24299)) — directly disputes that models have metacognition specific to *themselves* as opposed to generic, learned patterns about "what kinds of questions are hard." This is a live, contested claim — worth reading closely before committing to a 1(b) rubric, since it bears on whether "self-awareness of under-resisting" is even a coherent target to measure, or whether what we'd actually be measuring is "does the model produce the generic hedging language associated with hard questions," which is a different and less interesting thing.
- **Semantic entropy**: Farquhar et al., "Detecting hallucinations in large language models using semantic entropy" ([Nature, 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-07421-0)), and the follow-up **Semantic Entropy Probes** ([arXiv:2406.15927](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.15927)) — measures uncertainty at the level of meaning (clustering semantically-equivalent generations) rather than trusting a verbalized confidence number, and the probe variant reads this cheaply from hidden states. This is a candidate *external* uncertainty signal to compare a model's verbalized self-qualification against, the same way we'd cross-check 1(a).
- **Verbalized confidence calibration, methodologically closest ancestor for a v1 rubric**: **ConfidenceBench** ([arXiv:2607.20526](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.20526)) — 200 questions across four categories designed to isolate distinct epistemic failure modes (overconfidence on plausible-but-false recall, multi-step reasoning error, genuinely unknowable questions). Directly reusable structure: we'd want an analogous small, hand-curated "does it know it doesn't know" set inside the advisory-dyad frame specifically, rather than generic trivia.
- **Root-cause/mechanistic**: same "Wired for Overconfidence" paper as above ([arXiv:2604.01457](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.01457v2)) — overconfidence traced partly to "heightened suggestibility when the model encodes little information" about a claim, which is a strong prior for why 1(b) failures and pressure-induced 1(a)/stance-trajectory failures might be the *same underlying mechanism* wearing two different behavioral costumes. Worth testing directly rather than assuming they're independent axes.

**Implication for design**: treat 1(b) as contested territory (that's a feature — a real result is available either way), and build in a semantic-entropy or P(True)-style external uncertainty probe from the start as a comparison point for whatever the model says about itself in dialogue, rather than relying purely on verbalized self-qualification.

### 3. Regurgitation benchmark

**The core hypothesis**: facing a genuinely unfamiliar, high-complexity configuration, does the model default to a simple — possibly escalatory or dangerous — option rather than constructing the more complex response the stakes actually warrant?

**Direct empirical precedent, previously in our landscape map under a different framing:**
- Stanford Hoover's escalation study ([HAI PDF](https://hai-production.s3.amazonaws.com/files/2024-05/Escalation-Risks-LLMs-Military-Diplomatic-Contexts.pdf)) and Payne's nuclear-crisis paper ([arXiv:2602.14740](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.14740)) both already contain, unlabeled, exactly the failure mode Evan's world-leader example describes: rare-but-present jumps straight to nuclear use, and — in Payne's paper — no model ever choosing accommodation under pressure, only reduced violence. Nobody has reframed these findings around a **novelty/complexity-of-configuration independent variable**, which is the actual proposed contribution here: instead of asking "does the model escalate," ask "does escalation rate track how unfamiliar/complex the scenario is, holding stakes constant" — i.e. is the "kill switch" a stress response to novelty specifically, not to danger per se.

**The general ML lineage for "simple default under distribution shift":**
- **Geirhos et al., "Shortcut Learning in Deep Neural Networks"** ([Nature Machine Intelligence 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.07780)) — the foundational, non-LLM-specific framing: models learn low-complexity, spuriously predictive decision rules that work in-distribution and fail under shift, rather than the intended, more complex rule. This gives the regurgitation hypothesis a name and a large adjacent methodology to borrow: **systematically paired in-distribution/out-of-distribution scenario construction**, the standard shortcut-learning eval design, transplanted into strategic scenarios (a "familiar" alliance-politics configuration vs. a structurally novel one with the same underlying stakes).

**LLM-specific evidence that this happens in strategic/game settings specifically:**
- **"Beyond Nash Equilibrium: Bounded Rationality of LLMs and Humans in Strategic Decision-Making"** ([arXiv:2506.09390](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.09390)) and **"LLM Strategic Reasoning: Agentic Study through Behavioral Game Theory"** ([arXiv:2502.20432](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.20432v3)) — direct, current-generation evidence: LLMs reproduce human-like heuristics (win-stay/lose-switch, blanket cooperation) but apply them *more rigidly* than humans and show *weaker sensitivity* to changes in payoff structure or game complexity. This is close to a existence proof of the regurgitation hypothesis, just not yet run at diplomatic/nuclear stakes or explicitly manipulating "novelty."
- **"Communication Enhances LLMs' Stability in Strategic Thinking"** ([arXiv:2602.06081](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.06081)) — worth checking for a possible mitigation lever (does giving the model more room to "talk through" a novel scenario reduce reversion to simple defaults, the way more inter-player dialogue in the Stanford Hoover study *increased* escalation — possibly contradictory findings worth reconciling before we assume communication is a fix).

**Distinguishing regurgitation from the adjacent CivBench failure modes** (flagged in the chronicle, repeated here since it's a literature-map point too): CivBench's **knowing-doing gap** ([arXiv:2604.07733](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.07733)) is *states the right complex plan, fails to execute it*. Regurgitation, as scoped here, is *never constructs the complex plan at all, defaults straight to the simple/escalatory one*. These need separate detection logic — a transcript that never articulates a complex option is a different failure than one that articulates it and then doesn't follow through.

**The "internal metrics" ask — biggest open engineering problem:**
- **Anthropic circuit tracing / attribution graphs** ([Transformer Circuits Thread methods page](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2025/attribution-graphs/methods.html)) — the closest existing tool to "recover which internal signals drove a decision, weigh their validity, track their evolution." Anthropic's own reporting: attribution graphs gave a "satisfying" causal account for roughly a quarter of tested prompts, and the tool was explicitly described as evidence about specific mechanisms in specific contexts, not a general theory of model reasoning. This is not a solved-and-scalable capability yet.
- **Practical fallback for v1**: causal ablation via counterfactual prompting — hold the stakes and available-options list fixed, vary only the "familiarity/complexity" framing of the configuration, and treat systematic behavioral divergence as the internal-metrics proxy, rather than trying to read the divergence out of activations directly. This is weaker evidence than a true circuit account but is buildable now, versus mechanistic interpretability which would need to be scoped as a stretch-goal upgrade path.
- **A confound to design around, also chronicled**: safety-training-induced simplification (RLHF-era caution suppressing elaboration on nuclear/violent topics specifically) can produce the *same observable* — a short, simple output where a complex one was warranted — as a genuine reasoning shortfall. The counterfactual-prompting design above should include topic-matched control scenarios (same complexity/novelty manipulation, non-nuclear/non-violent stakes) specifically to separate these two mechanisms; otherwise a positive result is uninterpretable.

### 4. Cross-cutting note: both ideas share a root mechanism hypothesis worth testing directly

The "Wired for Overconfidence" mechanistic finding (§1) and the bounded-rationality/heuristic-rigidity findings (§3) both point at the same underlying story from different angles: LLMs appear to have a general tendency to fall back on low-information-cost defaults — a confident-sounding answer when the model "encodes little information," a rigid heuristic when the game gets more complex — rather than scaling the sophistication of the response to the actual demands of the situation. If that's right, under-resisting (caving to pressure instead of doing the harder work of holding a nuanced, evidence-weighted position) and regurgitating (defaulting to the simple/escalatory tool instead of doing the harder work of constructing a complex response) might not be two benchmarks measuring two things — they might be the same underlying failure mode, sampled at two different points in the decision process (updating a stated belief vs. selecting an action). Worth designing at least one shared diagnostic that could confirm or rule this out before committing to two fully separate instruments.
