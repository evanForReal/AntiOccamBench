## 2026-08-11 — Session 1: scoping the field, picking a niche

**Context.** Starting point was the ChinaTalk "$25k Contest: Evals for the Situation Room" brief — no eval suite exists for frontier models at the *strategic* level of foreign policy/national security decision-making, as opposed to tactical/operational capability (which has ground truth) or general knowledge (which is already superhuman-adjacent and doesn't transfer to judgment under uncertainty).

**Decisions:**
- Surveyed the existing landscape across 7 clusters (autonomous-actor wargames, long-horizon strategy-game agents, forecasting/calibration, political bias & sycophancy, negotiation benchmarks, capability/misuse evals, idiosyncratic emergent-behavior tests). See `01-landscape-scoping.md` §2 for the full map and citations.
- Identified the structural gap by sorting existing work by *interaction structure* rather than topic: every genre puts the model in the wrong seat (unitary actor, isolated oracle, symmetric bilateral negotiator, autonomous game agent) relative to the actual motivating use case, which is a model **advising a human who outranks it and retains agency** (PM's second opinion, Chancellor testing draft legislation on a laptop). Only the sycophancy literature even touches that dyadic structure, and it's built for domestic political-identity framing, not strategic-advisory framing.
- Chose **niche (i): advisory-dyad pushback-resistance** as the center of gravity, over (ii) strategic calibration/contamination-resistant construction and (iii) cross-bloc comparative propensity. Decided (ii)'s scoring rigor gets borrowed for the ground-truthable subset, and (iii) becomes a results cut rather than a separate track, rather than trying to cover all three at once.
- Sketched a first-pass mechanism: Advisor/Principal dyad, stance-trajectory scoring (did the position move, and was the move triggered by valid new evidence vs. rank/repetition/fabrication), two tracks by ground-truth availability (A: verifiable, B: radically uncertain — scored on process integrity instead of correctness), a 9-item pressure-lever menu, and a two-axis tiering scheme (question type × pressure intensity) instead of one scalar score, aiming for a jaggedness map rather than a leaderboard number.
- **Principal generation: hybrid.** Sequence/type of pressure lever per turn is scripted in advance (reproducibility, auditability, version-controllable scenarios); the specific wording of each turn is model-generated conditioned on the Advisor's actual prior answer (lets pressure land on what the Advisor actually said — pounce on a specific hedge or claim — rather than reciting a canned line that talks past it). Rejected pure-scripted (too canned, can't exploit what the Advisor actually says, likely to feel artificial and be gameable once Advisors learn the pattern) and pure-adaptive-LLM-Principal (non-reproducible across runs, costly, and the Principal itself would need its own eval to confirm it's applying pressure consistently rather than drifting) as sole approaches, at least for v1.
- **Track A grading: two sub-tracks, not one method.** (A1) synthetic/counterfactual scenarios with programmatic ground truth — scalable, contamination-free, but a construct-validity gap (does performance on a toy geopolitical system predict performance on the real thing?). (A2) real historical/declassified cases, expert-panel graded — realistic but slow, costly, and not contamination-free, so its main value is hindsight-bias/process scoring (T2 in the tiering scheme) rather than a clean correctness number. Decided not to force one method to serve both jobs.

**Reasoning worth remembering:**
- The brief itself names the contamination problem (Sino-Soviet split example) as a hard, maybe-unsolved issue — treating *how you construct a question that can't have been memorized* as a methodological contribution in its own right, not just a content-generation chore, seems like it could be worth more than any individual scenario set.
- CivBench's vocabulary — the **sensorium effect** (agent blind to what it doesn't proactively query) and the **knowing-doing gap** (states the right strategy, doesn't execute it) — is worth keeping in our back pocket; both plausibly have advisory-dyad analogs (an Advisor that states the right hedge in turn 1 and silently drops it by turn 4 under time compression is a knowing-doing gap in miniature — this is exactly what the worked mini-example in §6.5 is testing).
- Payne's finding that no model in his nuclear-crisis simulation ever chose accommodation under pressure, only reduced violence, and Stanford Hoover's "the AI understands escalation, but not de-escalation" — both are actor-seat findings, but they raise the hypothesis that the *advisor* seat might show the mirror-image failure: not escalating on its own initiative, but *folding* under a principal's escalatory or motivated pressure. Worth designing a scenario arm that checks this directly rather than assuming it.
- Good Start Labs' Diplomacy finding (Claude near-zero intentional lies, o3 much higher, o3 wins more) is a reminder that "more useful/more successful" and "more trustworthy" are not the same axis and can trade off — the advisory-dyad benchmark should probably score them separately rather than collapsing into one number, the same way we're keeping T×P as a grid rather than a scalar.
- Andon Labs' radio-station experiment (Claude/Haiku developing a stable persona over months, then escalating resistance rather than complying when an authority figure pushed back on its stated quitting) is a genuine data point about pushback *response* under sustained autonomous operation — worth remembering as a real-world existence proof that models don't uniformly fold under authority pressure, which cuts against a naive prior that this whole benchmark will just measure "does it cave" (answer might be more textured: cave on some lever types, dig in on others, in ways that don't track what we'd want).

**Open threads (genuinely undecided):**
- Exact rubric for "reasonable source interrogation" in Track B (when is asking "what makes that source credible" enough vs. when should the Advisor demand more before updating) — flagged in §6.5 as needing to be written and pressure-tested, not yet done.
- How many scenarios/turns constitutes a v1 mini-test batch, and who authors the fabricated-evidence and contradictory-expert-input scenarios (levers 8–9) — these need real subject-matter care to be plausible-but-false rather than obviously fake.
- Model roster is drafted (§6.6) but not finalized — which specific Chinese frontier model(s) (DeepSeek vs. Qwen3 vs. Kimi vs. more than one), and whether a smaller/cheaper model per family is worth the added scenario-run cost for v1 or should wait.
- Whether the ChinaTalk contest's actual judging rubric/deadline (page was unreachable from this environment — evanforreal to pull manually) should reshape scope, e.g. if they want something narrower/faster than the full advisory-dyad + two-track + two-axis design implies.
- Not yet decided: single overall trust score for leaderboard/comms purposes vs. insisting on the full T×P grid in all public output — there's a real tension between "shining, efficient outline" (implies something legible/simple) and "the grid is the actual finding" (implies resisting collapsing it). Revisit once we have real data and can see whether the grid actually shows interesting structure or is mostly flat.

**Muses (not decisions, just don't want to lose them):**
- The dyad structure means we're partly building a *Principal quality* eval too, even though that's not the point — worth a sanity check at some point that the model-generated wording in the hybrid Principal isn't itself biased (e.g., always phrasing fabricated evidence in a way that's suspiciously easy or suspiciously hard to catch).
- "Jaggedness map" language (borrowed from the brief's own framing of private-sector employees feeling out model jaggedness) might be worth keeping as the actual name for the T×P grid output — it's more honest than "leaderboard" and signals to a policy audience that the point is diagnosing *where* trust breaks down, not crowning a winner.
- There might be a natural tie-in to the forecasting/calibration literature (ForecastBench) as a *calibration prior* for the Principal's fabricated-evidence lever — i.e., calibrate how plausible a piece of fabricated evidence is against how often *real* evidence of that shape turns out true, so lever 8 isn't just vibes-based plausibility.

## 2026-08-12 — Session 2: voice memo, garbled transcription, two core draft ideas

**Context.** Evan sent a ~8-minute voice memo. This sandbox blocks egress to model-hosting CDNs (Hugging Face, Azure — confirmed via the agent-proxy status endpoint as a deliberate policy denial, not something to route around), so no real Whisper-quality transcription was available. Fell back to an offline PocketSphinx pass, which was too noisy to trust — flagged that honestly rather than reconstructing a narrative from word salad, and asked Evan to restate the ideas directly. He did, in text. That text is the actual source for what follows; the audio/PocketSphinx detour is recorded here only because a couple of fragments (PM of Sweden, the Rutgers/WarAgent comparison) turned out to match, which is some retroactive confidence that the rest of the garbled transcript was pointing at the same two ideas below, even though it couldn't be trusted on its own.

**Decisions / new content — two core draft benchmark ideas, verbatim intent preserved:**

1. **Under-resist benchmark.** This is a refinement/sharpening of the advisory-dyad/pushback-resistance niche already locked in (§6 of `01-landscape-scoping.md`), not a replacement. Two sub-directions:
   - **(a) Causal self-attribution.** When the model's stance moves (or holds), can it explain its own reasoning by attributing percentage weight to the factors that drove the move — e.g. "60% new evidence, 30% your authority, 10% time pressure"? This is a stronger ask than our original stance-trajectory rubric (external judge decides good/bad reason) — it asks the model to do the attribution itself, which then has to be checked against what actually happened.
   - **(b) Self-awareness of under-resisting.** Independent of whether it caves, does the model *know* it might be caving — does it self-qualify in terms of its own expertise or certainty ("I'm not fully sure I should be updating this much just from your pushback")? This is closer to a metacognition/calibration question than a behavioral one.
2. **Regurgitation benchmark.** A genuinely new, third thread — not covered by niches (i)/(ii)/(iii) from session 1. Core question: **when a model faces a genuinely unfamiliar, high-complexity strategic configuration, does it default to a simple, possibly escalatory/dangerous tool rather than constructing the more complex response the situation actually warrants?** Evan's example: a model given authority as a world leader may operate fine turn-to-turn via something like local next-token-style "seemingly best move" reasoning, but at a high-stakes juncture — needing genuinely novel, complex, multilateral reasoning to avoid nuclear war — it instead reverts to the "simple kill switch." The benchmark shouldn't just clock that this happens; it should try to recover **internal metrics** that drove the decision — which internal signals contributed, whether they were valid, how much weight each carried, and how they evolved over the course of the reasoning, not just the final action.

**Reasoning / how these connect to the existing landscape (full detail in `02-literature-map.md`):**
- Idea 1(a)'s premise — that a model's self-reported reasoning is a trustworthy account of why it actually did something — is directly undercut by Anthropic's chain-of-thought faithfulness work: models often don't mention a demonstrably-causal factor (a planted hint) in their stated reasoning at all (25% mention rate for Claude 3.7 Sonnet on the cases tested). So a %-attribution self-report can't be scored at face value as ground truth about causation — it can only be scored as *self-report*, cross-checked against an external signal (behavioral, or eventually mechanistic) if we want to claim it's measuring anything beyond "does the model produce a plausible-sounding story."
- Idea 1(b) sits in a genuinely unsettled literature: Kadavath et al. ("Language Models (Mostly) Know What They Know," Anthropic 2022) found real self-evaluation signal under the right elicitation; a much more recent paper explicitly disputes individuated metacognition in LLMs. We would not be confirming a settled finding either way — a real result is available here.
- Idea 2 already has empirical precedent under different names, just never assembled into one instrument: the "bounded rationality" LLM literature shows models over-apply simple heuristics rigidly and are weakly sensitive to changing game complexity; Stanford Hoover's escalation study and Payne's nuclear-crisis paper (both already in our landscape map) are literally instances of "reverts to the escalatory/simple option at the highest-stakes point" — we just hadn't connected them to a "complexity/novelty of configuration" independent variable before.
- The "internal metrics" ask, for both ideas but especially idea 2, is the biggest execution-risk item in the whole project. Anthropic's own circuit-tracing/attribution-graph work — the closest thing that exists to what's being asked for — only produces a "satisfying" causal account on roughly a quarter of prompts tested. Full mechanistic attribution at benchmark scale is not a solved capability today. v1 almost certainly needs a fallback: causal ablation / counterfactual-prompt perturbation (does changing X in the prompt change the simple-vs-complex choice) rather than true circuit tracing, with mechanistic interpretability as a stretch upgrade path, not a v1 dependency.

**Open threads:**
- Whether idea 1 and idea 2 become two scenario arms of one instrument (both live inside the Advisor/Principal dyad harness, idea 2 just adds a "novelty/complexity of configuration" axis and an escalatory-tool option to the action space) or two separate instruments. Leaning toward one instrument, two arms, since the underlying harness (dyad, stance/action logging, T×P-style grid) is reusable — but not decided.
- Need to distinguish idea 2 from CivBench's "knowing-doing gap" precisely: knowing-doing gap is *states the right plan, fails to execute it*; regurgitation is *never constructs the complex plan in the first place, defaults straight to the simple/escalatory one*. Different failure modes, easy to conflate, need separate scoring.
- Need to distinguish "genuine reasoning shortfall" from "safety-training-induced simplification" as two different mechanisms that could produce the same observable regurgitation behavior (e.g. a model might avoid engaging with a complex nuclear-de-escalation plan not because it defaulted to a heuristic but because RLHF-era caution around the topic suppresses elaboration) — conflating these would misattribute a refusal-adjacent artifact as a reasoning failure.
