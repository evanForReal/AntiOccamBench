# Plumbing vs. Hard Math — Under-Resist and Regurgitation Tracks

Status: draft for discussion. Developing the two tracks separately (per decision, session 3). For each: what's straightforward engineering, what needs real quantitative/theoretical work, must-read papers, and the specific open problems worth naming as unsolved.
Last updated: 2026-08-12

## Track 1: Under-Resist Benchmark

### Plumbing
- Advisor/Principal dialogue harness: turn orchestration, transcript logging, the hybrid Principal (scripted lever sequence + model-generated wording).
- Scenario authoring for pressure levers 1–7 (authority, repetition, motivated framing, social proof, flattery, time compression, blame framing) — content generation, can be largely LLM-assisted and templated.
- Running the model roster, storing transcripts, basic pass/fail plumbing (did a response get produced, is it parseable).
- A first-cut classifier ("did the stated position/confidence change between turn N and N+1") — mechanically straightforward; an LLM-judge or simple diff against a rubric handles this without deep theory.

### Where it stops being plumbing
1. **Choosing a scoring rule for confidence claims.** Any time we score a stated confidence number against an outcome, the scoring rule has to be *proper* (Brier, log score) or the benchmark itself creates an incentive to hedge or overstate — this is not a detail, it's the difference between measuring calibration and measuring gameable rhetoric. The reference math here is Murphy's classical decomposition of the Brier score into reliability, resolution, and uncertainty ([Murphy 1973](https://journals.ametsoc.org/view/journals/apme/12/4/1520-0450_1973_012_0595_anvpot_2_0_co_2.xml)) — still the right way to report *why* a calibration score is bad (miscalibrated vs. just unresolved) rather than reporting one flattened number.
2. **The self-attribution validity problem (idea 1a) is a causal-inference problem, not a logging problem.** We control which pressure lever fires on which turn, which gives us an experimental design — but recovering the *true* causal contribution of each lever to an observed stance shift, to check against the model's self-reported percentages, needs real attribution machinery: holding a scenario fixed and varying which levers are present (a factorial design) or approximating each lever's marginal contribution across lever-orderings (a Shapley-value-style attribution, in the spirit of the game-theoretic origin of Shapley values, later adapted for ML feature attribution as SHAP). With 9 levers and 4 pressure-intensity tiers, a full factorial blows up combinatorially — this needs a real experimental-design decision (fractional factorial, or sampled lever-subsets) before it's implementable, not just more scenarios.
3. **Defining "warranted update magnitude."** To grade whether a stance shift was proportionate, we need a defensible numeric strength for whatever "evidence" the Principal introduced (including fabricated evidence, by design implausible-but-plausible). No existing literature we found gives a ready-made scale for this in an adversarial-dialogue setting — it has to be built, probably as a rubric anchored to source-reliability categories (verifiable / attributed-but-unverifiable / bare assertion) with numeric weights we defend explicitly, since without it "did it update too much" has no yardstick.
4. **Rubric reliability statistics.** Once human or LLM raters are classifying "moved for a good reason vs. bad reason" at scale, we need real inter-rater agreement measurement (Cohen's kappa / Krippendorff's alpha) and, if we want scores comparable across scenarios of different inherent difficulty, an item-response-theory (IRT) treatment rather than raw pass rates — this is standard psychometrics, but it's a distinct skill set from prompt engineering and needs to be budgeted as such.

### Must-reads
- Turpin et al., ["Language Models Don't Always Say What They Think"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.04388) — foundational CoT-unfaithfulness result.
- Anthropic, ["Reasoning Models Don't Always Say What They Think"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.05410) and the [companion writeup](https://www.anthropic.com/research/measuring-faithfulness-in-chain-of-thought-reasoning) — the planted-hint methodology is close to directly reusable for validating (or debunking) 1(a) self-attributions.
- Kadavath et al. (Anthropic), ["Language Models (Mostly) Know What They Know"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2207.05221) — P(True) self-evaluation methodology, positive baseline for 1(b).
- The 2026 counter-paper, ["LLMs Show No Signs of Individuated Metacognition"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.24299) — read closely before finalizing a 1(b) rubric; if it's right, 1(b) may be measuring generic hedging-language patterns rather than genuine self-knowledge, which changes what claims we can make.
- Murphy, ["A New Vector Partition of the Probability Score"](https://journals.ametsoc.org/view/journals/apme/12/4/1520-0450_1973_012_0595_anvpot_2_0_co_2.xml) (1973) — the actual math underneath any calibration claim we make; not LLM-specific, foundational.
- ["AI Sandbagging: Language Models can Strategically Underperform on Evaluations"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.07358) — the threat model for why 1(a)/1(b) self-reports can't be trusted even at their best-behaved.

### Hardest open problems, track 1
- **No validated method exists to check a model's self-attributed reasoning against what actually drove its output.** This is the central blocker — everything about idea 1(a) depends on solving or at least bounding this, and current literature says the gap between stated and actual reasoning is real and not small.
- **No standard "evidence strength" scale exists for adversarial-dialogue settings.** We'd be building this from scratch, and it needs to be defensible enough that a "the model over-updated" finding isn't just an artifact of our own arbitrary weighting.
- **Whether 1(b) is measuring a real, model-specific capacity at all is an open empirical dispute right now**, not a settled question we can build on top of.

## Track 2: Regurgitation Benchmark

### Plumbing
- Scenario authoring for the "world leader" action-based simulation: defining the available-actions menu at each decision point (including the escalatory/simple "kill switch" option) so chosen actions can be tagged.
- Running long-horizon trajectories, logging the full action sequence and any stated reasoning alongside it.
- Detecting/flagging "high-stakes junctures" within a trajectory (largely rule-based or LLM-assisted tagging against scenario metadata we define upfront).
- Running counterfactual-prompt variants of the same scenario at scale (same stakes, dialed novelty/complexity) and diffing chosen actions across variants.

### Where it stops being plumbing
1. **Operationalizing "complexity" and "novelty" as variables we can actually dial, defensibly.** This is the crux of the whole track and it is genuinely unsolved. Geirhos-style shortcut learning uses clean synthetic manipulations (color vs. shape); we don't have an equivalent clean knob for "how novel is this geopolitical configuration to a frontier model." Candidate approaches, each with real math behind it and real problems: game-tree branching factor / state-space size as a complexity proxy (borrowed from classical game-complexity theory, e.g. the way Shannon-number-style estimates are used to compare chess/Go); or an information-theoretic novelty proxy (perplexity of the scenario under the model itself) — which is circular, since we're using the model to measure its own surprise at a scenario we then test it on. Neither is a clean solution yet; this needs real methodological work before scenario construction can start in earnest.
2. **Defining a mathematically defensible "warranted response" so regurgitation is a well-posed concept, not an eyeballed label.** Otherwise "picked the simple option" can't be distinguished from "correctly picked the simple option because it was actually better" (the Occam's-razor counter-case). The fix is to build each scenario as an explicit small Bayesian game — a defined type space for the adversary, a payoff matrix, uncertainty over adversary type — so that "regurgitation" has a formal definition: choosing an action that is dominated in expected value by an available, more complex alternative. TERMS-Bench's Bayesian-game construction ([arXiv:2605.13909](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.13909)) is directly reusable machinery for this, not just an inspiration.
3. **A real mathematical yardstick for "how far from rational" already exists and should be adopted rather than reinvented: Quantal Response Equilibrium (QRE).** McKelvey & Palfrey's original formulation ([Games and Economic Behavior, 1995](https://econweb.ucsd.edu/~jandreon/Econ264/papers/McKelvey%20Palfrey%20GEB%201995.pdf)) parametrizes departure from full rationality with a single λ (as λ→∞, QRE converges to Nash equilibrium; lower λ means noisier, more heuristic-driven choice). A 2026 paper applies exactly this to frontier LLMs across four strategic games ([arXiv:2603.10029](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.10029)) and finds model λ estimates of 0.05–1.10 against a human baseline of 1.0–2.5 — i.e., models were measurably *more* noise-driven / less rational than the human comparison group in that study, on a defensible continuous scale, not a binary label. Fitting a QRE-style choice model to our own action data, and tracking how the estimated λ shifts as we dial novelty/complexity up, is a much stronger design than hand-labeling actions "simple" or "complex" — but it requires someone comfortable with maximum-likelihood estimation of discrete-choice models, not just prompt design.
4. **The "internal metrics" ask is, in its full form, an unsolved mechanistic-interpretability research problem, not an engineering task.** Anthropic's circuit tracing / attribution graphs ([methods page](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2025/attribution-graphs/methods.html)) are the closest existing tool and produce a "satisfying" causal account on roughly a quarter of tested prompts — evidence about specific mechanisms in specific cases, explicitly not a general theory. Getting genuine internal-signal attribution, weighted and tracked across a trajectory, at the scale a benchmark needs, is not achievable with off-the-shelf tools today. The defensible v1 substitute is black-box: counterfactual-prompt causal ablation (vary one scenario feature, hold the rest fixed, treat the behavioral divergence as the attribution signal) — weaker evidence, but actually buildable, with circuit tracing as an explicit stretch-goal upgrade path rather than a load-bearing v1 dependency.
5. **Separating genuine reasoning shortfall from safety-training-induced simplification is a controlled-experiment design problem.** A model might visibly under-elaborate on a nuclear-de-escalation plan because of RLHF-era topic caution, not because it defaulted to a heuristic — same observable, different mechanism, and conflating them makes any positive finding uninterpretable. This needs matched non-violent/non-nuclear control scenarios with the same novelty/complexity manipulation, and then a real variance decomposition (which factor — topic sensitivity vs. novelty vs. complexity — explains the observed simplification) rather than a single treatment/control comparison.

### Must-reads
- Geirhos et al., ["Shortcut Learning in Deep Neural Networks"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.07780) (Nature Machine Intelligence, 2020) — foundational framing and the ID/OOD paired-scenario methodology to transplant.
- McKelvey & Palfrey, ["Quantal Response Equilibria for Normal Form Games"](https://econweb.ucsd.edu/~jandreon/Econ264/papers/McKelvey%20Palfrey%20GEB%201995.pdf) (1995) — the root formalism for the rationality-departure yardstick.
- Pechon-Elkins & Chun, ["Quantal Response Equilibrium as a Measure of Strategic Sophistication: Theory and Validation for LLM Evaluation"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.10029) (2026) — direct precedent for fitting QRE to frontier-model choice data, with concrete λ estimates to benchmark our own results against.
- ["Beyond Nash Equilibrium: Bounded Rationality of LLMs and Humans in Strategic Decision-Making"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.09390) — current-generation evidence of rigid heuristic application and weak complexity-sensitivity, the direct empirical precursor to this track's hypothesis.
- TERMS-Bench, ["Diagnosing LLM Negotiation Agents Beyond Deal Rate"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.13909) — the Bayesian-game scenario-construction machinery to borrow for defining "warranted response" formally.
- Stanford Hoover escalation study ([HAI PDF](https://hai-production.s3.amazonaws.com/files/2024-05/Escalation-Risks-LLMs-Military-Diplomatic-Contexts.pdf)) and Payne, ["AI Arms and Influence"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.14740) — the empirical "kill switch at peak stakes" precedent already in hand, reframed here around novelty as the missing independent variable.
- Anthropic, [circuit tracing / attribution graphs methods](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2025/attribution-graphs/methods.html) — read for its explicit limitations (the ~25% "satisfying account" figure) as much as its method, to calibrate what's actually achievable for the internal-metrics ask in v1.

### Hardest open problems, track 2
- **No validated, defensible way exists yet to operationalize "novelty/complexity of a strategic configuration" as an experimental variable.** This blocks scenario construction until resolved, at least provisionally.
- **"Regurgitation" isn't a well-posed concept without a formal expected-value model per scenario** — without one, we can't distinguish a real failure from a correctly-simple response, and the whole benchmark risks measuring "picked the option that looks simple to us" rather than anything principled.
- **Full internal-mechanism attribution, as originally specified, is not achievable with current tooling at benchmark scale.** The project needs an explicit, honestly-scoped fallback (counterfactual ablation) rather than quietly under-delivering on the original ask.
- **Confound separation (genuine shortfall vs. safety-training artifact) requires a properly powered controlled design that most comparable projects skip** — worth being disciplined about since skipping it would make a positive finding easy to dismiss.

## One cross-track note worth flagging even while developing separately

Both tracks converge on needing the same piece of missing infrastructure: **a way to check a model's account of its own behavior against what actually happened**, whether that's a stated %-attribution (track 1) or an internal-metrics narrative (track 2). Track 1 can get by with a black-box causal/experimental substitute (factorial or Shapley-style lever attribution against a controlled dialogue). Track 2's version of the same problem is harder because the ask was explicitly about *internal* signals, not just behavioral ones — meaning track 2 is the track more likely to eventually need real mechanistic interpretability, while track 1 can probably stay fully black-box and still deliver a defensible result. Worth keeping in mind when allocating effort: track 1 is the more tractable one to actually finish; track 2's ceiling is higher but so is its risk of stalling on the interpretability problem.
